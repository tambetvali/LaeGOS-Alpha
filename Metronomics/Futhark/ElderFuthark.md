# Germanic Runic Alphabets — Elder Futhark, Younger Futhark, Anglo-Saxon Futhorc and Medieval Futhork

## 1. Introduction

Runes were writing characters used by Germanic-speaking peoples, particularly in Northern and Western Europe. They served as phonetic writing signs, inscriptions, personal marks, memorial symbols and, in some contexts, objects of ritual or symbolic significance.

The historical development of runic writing is not a single linear evolution. Different Germanic populations adapted the runic system to their languages, producing several related but distinct alphabets.

The four principal traditions examined here are:

1. **Elder Futhark** — the early 24-rune Germanic system.
2. **Younger Futhark** — the 16-rune Scandinavian system of the Viking Age.
3. **Anglo-Saxon / Anglo-Frisian Futhorc** — an expanded system adapted to Old English and Old Frisian.
4. **Medieval Futhork** — later Scandinavian runic writing with additional phonetic distinctions.

The word *Futhark* derives from the first six characters:

ᚠ ᚢ ᚦ ᚨ ᚱ ᚲ

The Old English form *Futhorc* reflects the development of the fourth rune's sound and name.

An important distinction throughout this article is between:

- Glyph: the written rune.
- Phoneme: the sound represented.
- Rune name: the historical name of the character.
- Lexical meaning: the ordinary meaning of that name.
- Cultural interpretation: associations found in historical contexts.
- Esoteric interpretation: later symbolic and magical developments.

These layers should not be treated as interchangeable.

# 3. Younger Futhark — The 16-rune Viking alphabet

Younger Futhark developed in Scandinavia around the late eighth century. It became the principal runic writing system of the Viking Age.

It is especially important for interpreting Viking inscriptions and Scandinavian cultural history.

Unlike Elder Futhark, it contains only 16 runes.

This reduction did not mean that the language itself became simpler. Rather, the writing system used fewer characters to represent a greater range of sounds.

For example, a single rune could represent several related consonants or vowels.

# 4. Long-branch and short-twig Younger Futhark

Younger Futhark developed two major graphic styles.

They are not different alphabets in the fundamental sense. They represent different regional and practical approaches to writing the same 16-rune system.

| Feature | Long-branch | Short-twig |
|---|---|---|
| Traditional association | Danish | Swedish-Norwegian |
| Character style | More elaborate branches | Simplified branches |
| Typical context | Monumental inscriptions | Everyday inscriptions and objects |
| Alphabet | Younger Futhark | Younger Futhark |
| Phonetic system | Essentially shared | Essentially shared |

Long-branch runes are often associated with large runestones and carefully executed inscriptions.

Short-twig runes are more economical in their strokes and convenient for practical carving.

There were also transitional and regional variants.

# 5. Anglo-Saxon and Anglo-Frisian Futhorc

The Anglo-Saxon runic system developed from the earlier Germanic tradition.

Unlike Scandinavian runic writing, which reduced the number of characters, Anglo-Saxon writing expanded its inventory.

The expansion reflected the phonetic requirements of Old English and related languages.

The name Futhorc reflects the development of the fourth rune from the older A-associated form into an O-associated form.

# 6. Medieval Scandinavian Futhork

The Younger Futhark continued evolving after the Viking Age.

During the medieval period, dotted runes and other additional forms helped distinguish sounds that had previously shared the same character.

Examples include:

| Earlier rune | Dotted development | Distinction |
|---|---|---|
| ᚴ | ᚵ | K / G |
| ᛏ | ᛑ | T / D |
| ᛒ | ᛔ | B / P |
| ᚠ | ᚡ | F / V |
| ᚦ | ᚧ | Þ / ð |
| ᛁ | ᛂ | I / E |

The exact inventory and use of these characters varied.

Medieval Scandinavian runic writing eventually incorporated more distinctions and sometimes Latin letters.

This illustrates a recurring principle in writing-system evolution:

A small character inventory can represent many sounds through context, but later writers may introduce additional signs when more explicit distinctions become useful.

1. Old English Rune Poem.
2. Norwegian Rune Poem.
3. Icelandic Rune Poem.

These sources are valuable because they preserve descriptions beyond the simple phonetic values of the characters.

However, they are not contemporary records of the earliest Elder Futhark period.

# 12. Structural comparison: 24, 16 and 29+ runes

The number of characters provides an interesting mathematical perspective.

| System | Rune count | Possible organization |
|---|---:|---|
| Elder Futhark | 24 | 3 × 8 |
| Younger Futhark | 16 | 2 × 8 |
| Old English Futhorc | 29 | 3 × 8 + 5 |
| Expanded Futhorc | 30–33+ | Additional phonetic characters |
| Medieval Futhork | Variable | Expanded distinctions |

Elder Futhark has a particularly clear three-part organization.

Younger Futhark preserves the group-of-eight relationship only partially, with a distribution of 6 + 5 + 5.

Anglo-Saxon Futhorc retains the original 24-character foundation and expands it to accommodate additional phonetic distinctions.

These numerical structures can be studied independently of later symbolic interpretations.

They do not, by themselves, prove that the historical systems encoded a numerical cosmology.

# 16. Conclusion

The historical runic systems reveal several different approaches to the same broad writing tradition.

- Elder Futhark established a 24-character Germanic system.
- Younger Futhark compressed the Scandinavian system to 16 characters.
- Anglo-Saxon Futhorc expanded the system to represent additional linguistic distinctions.
- Medieval Futhork developed additional distinctions and adapted to changing writing practices.

The most important lesson is that a rune is simultaneously a written character, a sound representation and a historical linguistic object. Its name may preserve an older word, while its cultural and symbolic interpretation may change over time.

For comparative research, the most productive approach is to preserve these layers independently:

**Glyph → Sound → Name → Meaning → Cultural context → Poetic interpretation → Later symbolism.**

This makes it possible to investigate both the historical writing systems and their broader conceptual structures without confusing documented history with modern reconstruction.

# Elder Futhark — Rune Magic, Norse Mythology and the Laegna Cosmological Framework

## 1. Introduction

Elder Futhark is more than an ancient writing system in the sense that its characters were associated with words, names, natural phenomena, gods, animals, social relationships and the material world.

The 24 runes form a historically recognizable Germanic alphabet, but their names and associations also provide a rich foundation for comparative symbolic research.

This article explores five interconnected layers:

1. **Historical runology:** the origins, sounds and meanings of the 24 Elder Futhark runes.
2. **Rune magic:** historical evidence for magical and ritual uses of runes.
3. **Norse mythology:** gods, cosmology, Yggdrasil, creation, destruction and regeneration.

5. **Laegna interpretation:** comparative structures involving zero, one, infinity, symmetry, inversion, internal and external dimensions.

The Laegna layer is an exploratory philosophical and mathematical interpretation. It is not an established historical interpretation of Elder Futhark.

The central idea is to investigate whether runic symbolism can be organized into a coherent model of reality:

**Origin → Differentiation → Interaction → Transformation → Integration → Renewal.**

# 3. Rune magic and historical practice

## 3.1 What did rune magic mean?

Runes were primarily writing characters. They appeared on:

- Memorial stones.
- Weapons.
- Jewellery.
- Tools.
- Personal objects.
- Amulets.
- Wooden objects.
- Monumental inscriptions.

Some inscriptions contain apparent magical formulas, protective expressions, repeated characters or names associated with ritual contexts.

Historical evidence indicates that runes could be used in magical practices, but it does not establish one universal Germanic rune-magic system.

The later standardized system of 24 magical meanings, divinatory readings and reversed interpretations is largely a modern development.

## 3.2 The myth of Odin and the runes

One of the most important sources is the Old Norse poem *Hávamál*, particularly the section commonly called Rúnatal.

In this account, Odin describes a self-sacrifice on the world-tree Yggdrasil:

- He hangs on the tree.
- He is wounded by a spear.
- He experiences a period of suffering.
- He obtains the runes.
- He gains knowledge and power through this experience.

The myth describes knowledge as something acquired through sacrifice, endurance and transformation.

This is not simply the invention of an alphabet.

It is a mythological representation of the relationship between suffering, insight, sacrifice and knowledge.

The runes become associated with hidden knowledge, speech, memory, power and the ability to influence the world.

## 3.3 Galdr and seiðr

Two important concepts in Norse magical traditions are:

| Concept | General meaning | Context |
|---|---|---|
| Galdr | Incantation or magical chanting | Spoken or sung formulas |
| Seiðr | A form of ritual magic | Prophecy, fate, altered states and influence |

Galdr is especially relevant to the relationship between sound, speech and runes.

A written rune can represent a phoneme, but it can also be part of a name, inscription or formula.

Seiðr is a broader ritual practice and should not be reduced to rune manipulation.

## 3.4 Historical and modern distinctions

| Practice | Historical status |
|---|---|
| Writing names and messages in runes | Well documented |
| Memorial and ownership inscriptions | Well documented |
| Some magical inscriptions and formulas | Documented |
| Repeated runes and ritual-looking inscriptions | Documented, interpretation varies |
| Odin's acquisition of runes | Mythological source |
| Standardized 24-rune divination system | Predominantly modern |
| Universal reversed meanings | Modern interpretative convention |
| One universal ancient rune cosmology | Not established |

# 4. Norse cosmology and Yggdrasil

Yggdrasil, the world-tree, is one of the central images of Norse mythology.

It connects different realms of existence and is associated with gods, humans, giants, the dead, animals and cosmic processes.

The surviving literary sources do not give one perfectly consistent map of all the worlds. The familiar nine-world diagram is a useful modern synthesis, not a single definitive ancient chart.

## 4.1 Yggdrasil as a relational structure

Yggdrasil can be interpreted as a structure connecting:

- Different levels of existence.
- Different kinds of beings.
- Different temporal conditions.
- Different sources of knowledge.
- Different forms of transformation.

The tree is not merely a vertical ladder.

It is a network of relationships.

Its roots, trunk, branches and surrounding beings form a system in which different regions influence one another.

# 5. The Laegna philosophical framework

For this comparison, we introduce several Laegna concepts as analytical tools.

They are not claimed to be ancient runic doctrines.

## 5.1 Zero — the undefined or structural origin

Zero can represent a boundary between distinguishable existence and an undifferentiated reference condition.

In Laegna-oriented thinking, it is useful to distinguish:

- Numerical zero.
- Absence of a quantity.
- Structural origin.
- Undefined or unrepresented state.
- A boundary from which distinctions become meaningful.

A rune cannot simply be equated with zero because Elder Futhark does not have a zero rune.

Instead, zero can be used to examine what happens before the distinctions represented by runic signs emerge.

A useful question is:

What must exist before there can be a difference between one thing and another?

This is a philosophical question, not an established answer supplied by runology.

## 5.2 One — unity and the first distinction

One represents a distinguishable entity or reference.

It can represent:

- A single object.
- An individual.
- A unit of measurement.
- A point of reference.
- A defined state.

In the runic context, a single rune is already a differentiated sign.

Its identity depends on its contrast with other possible signs.

Thus, the alphabet can be studied as a system of distinctions rather than as 24 independent objects.

## 5.3 Infinity — the continuation of possible structures

Infinity is not represented by a specific Elder Futhark rune.

It can nevertheless be used as a mathematical and philosophical concept for studying:

- Unbounded extension.
- Repeating cycles.
- Potentially unlimited transformations.
- Recursive structures.
- Infinite relationships between scales.

For example:

- Jēra represents a year and recurring cycles.
- Eihwaz is associated with a long-lived tree.
- Dagaz represents daylight.
- Ōþala represents inherited continuity.

These provide different historical concepts that can be compared with temporal and structural infinity.

They should not be equated with mathematical infinity itself.

## 5.4 Inside and outside

One of the most productive Laegna comparisons is the relationship between internal and external structures.

| Dimension | Internal interpretation | External interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Space | Containment | Expansion |
| Structure | Internal relations | External relations |
| Knowledge | Inner organization | Observable manifestation |
| Geometry | Enclosure | Projection |
| Time | Stored state | Process |
| Identity | Internal coherence | External distinction |

This provides a way to compare runic concepts such as:

- Ice and water.
- Roots and branches.
- Wealth and exchange.
- Stillness and movement.
- Inheritance and expansion.
- Hidden knowledge and spoken knowledge.

The inside/outside distinction is a modern analytical lens, not an ancient runic classification.

# 11. Zero, one, infinity and the runic system

This section develops a more explicitly mathematical comparison.

The runic alphabet itself has no zero character.

Nevertheless, the concepts of zero, one and infinity can help construct a comparative model.

## 11.1 Zero — the absence of differentiation

Zero can be used to represent a state in which a particular distinction has not yet been made.

This does not mean that zero is identical to nothingness.

In mathematics, zero has a well-defined role, while an undefined value, an empty set and an unobserved state are different concepts.

For Laegna, it is useful to distinguish:

| Concept | Mathematical interpretation |
|---|---|
| 0 | Numerical zero |
| ∅ | Empty set |
| Undefined | No defined value |
| Unknown | Value not yet known |
| Origin | Reference from which distinctions are measured |
| Potential | Possible future states |

In a symbolic model, the transition from an undifferentiated condition to a differentiated one can be represented as:

$$
0 \rightarrow 1
$$

The runic system begins with distinct signs, so it represents an already differentiated symbolic world.

The philosophical question is what precedes the distinction.

## 11.2 One — a defined entity

One represents a unit or distinguishable object.

For example:

- One rune.
- One object.
- One individual.
- One point.
- One unit of measurement.

A single sign is meaningful because it can be distinguished from other signs.

Thus, identity and difference emerge together.

$$
1 \neq 2
$$

But one can also become the reference for constructing larger systems.

## 11.3 Infinity — unbounded continuation

Infinity can be interpreted through several different structures:

- Infinite sequences.
- Infinite extension.
- Repeating cycles.
- Recursive processes.
- Unlimited possible states.

Jēra, Dagaz and the recurring cycles of Norse cosmology provide symbolic material for comparison with periodicity.

However, a repeating cycle is not itself mathematical infinity.

A finite periodic process can repeat indefinitely in an ideal mathematical model.

## 11.4 A possible Laegna sequence

A useful abstract sequence is:

$$
0 \rightarrow 1 \rightarrow 2 \rightarrow 4
\rightarrow 8 \rightarrow 16 \rightarrow \cdots
$$

This sequence represents repeated binary differentiation or doubling.

It can be compared philosophically with the emergence of distinctions, relationships and increasing structural complexity.

It is not an encoded numerical sequence in Elder Futhark.

The comparison is useful because it separates:

- Origin.
- First distinction.
- Duality.
- Multiplicity.
- Expansion.
- Increasing complexity.

# 15. Norse mythology and the cosmic cycle

Norse mythology contains a powerful relationship between creation, existence, destruction and renewal.

The most important example is Ragnarök.

Ragnarök is the series of events involving the destruction of the established cosmic order and the deaths of many gods.

But the mythology does not end with destruction.

The world emerges again, and surviving figures participate in a renewed existence.

## 15.1 Creation and destruction

| Mythological event | Symbolic interpretation | Laegna comparison |
|---|---|---|
| Primordial Ginnungagap | Open primordial space | Undifferentiated condition |

| Yggdrasil | Connected existence | Relational system |

| Renewed world | New emergence | Reorganization |

Ginnungagap is a primordial void or gap in Norse cosmology, but it should not be equated directly with mathematical zero.

\text{Primordial condition}

\rightarrow
\text{Organization}

\rightarrow
\text{Destruction}
\rightarrow
\text{Renewal}
$$

This resembles a cyclic model rather than a purely linear model of history.

Jēra and Dagaz provide additional rune-based comparisons for recurring temporal processes.

# 18. Conclusion — From runic writing to structural philosophy

Elder Futhark offers an unusually rich foundation for comparative research.

Its characters preserve connections with material wealth, animals, natural phenomena, human relationships, gods, time, social structures and inherited continuity.

Norse mythology adds a broader cosmological context through Yggdrasil, Odin, the Norns, primordial creation and Ragnarök.

Rune magic adds another dimension through the historical use of signs, words, names and inscriptions in ritual contexts.

Laegna provides a separate mathematical and philosophical framework through which these subjects can be compared.

The most productive correspondences are not necessarily direct one-to-one translations.

They are structural relationships:

- Fehu and Gebō: possession and exchange.
- Uruz and Þurisaz: potential and opposition.
- Ansuz and Mannaz: information and interpretation.
- Raidō and Ehwaz: movement and coordination.
- Hagalaz and Nauðiz: disturbance and constraint.
- Īsaz and Laguz: internal order and continuous transformation.
- Jēra and Dagaz: cycles and transitions.
- Eihwaz and Ōþala: persistence and continuity.
- Ingwaz and Berkanan: potential and emergence.
- Tīwaz and Wunjō: order and harmony.

The three ættir can also be investigated as an abstract progression:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Emergence}
\rightarrow
\text{Transformation}
\rightarrow
\text{Integration}
}
$$

The deepest philosophical connection is perhaps the relationship between unity and differentiation.

A system becomes understandable through distinctions, yet its elements remain connected through relationships.

The individual rune is distinct, but the alphabet is a system.

The individual tree is distinct, but Yggdrasil represents interconnected existence.

The individual human is distinct, but humanity exists through social relationships.

And in Laegna, the distinction between internal and external, zero and one, finite and infinite, can be investigated as a set of structural relationships rather than isolated opposites.

**The central research proposition:**

A symbolic system can be studied simultaneously as language, cultural memory, information structure and a model of relationships. Elder Futhark provides the historical material; Norse mythology provides a cosmological narrative; Laegna provides a modern mathematical language for exploring possible structural parallels.

The objective is not to prove that the ancient Germanic peoples possessed Laegna mathematics, but to investigate whether different symbolic traditions can reveal recurring patterns in how humans understand existence, change, symmetry and continuity.

## Methodological note

Historical inscriptions, reconstructed rune names, medieval literary sources and modern esoteric interpretations have different levels of evidential reliability.

For further Laegna research, each symbolic correspondence should ideally be stored with:

- Its historical source.
- Its linguistic origin.
- Its period.
- Its confidence level.
- Its modern interpretative layer.
- Its Laegna structural mapping.

This preserves the distinction between historical knowledge and new theoretical development.
