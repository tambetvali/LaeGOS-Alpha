# 1. The below-one reference

The first important value below unity is:

$$
0.125=\frac18=2^{-3}
$$

This number has an exact binary-octave interpretation.

$$
\boxed{\log_2(0.125)=-3}
$$

Therefore, 0.125 is not an arbitrary decimal subdivision. It is the third negative octave of the binary scale.

We can define the first-order scale coordinate as:

$$
X_1(n)=2^n
$$

For negative positions:

$$
X_1(-1)=0.5
$$

$$
X_1(-2)=0.25
$$

$$
X_1(-3)=0.125
$$

$$
X_1(-4)=0.0625
$$

The corresponding reciprocal values are:

$$
X_1(-n)=\frac{1}{X_1(n)}
$$

This creates a symmetric relationship around unity:

$$
X_1(n)X_1(-n)=1
$$

Unity is the reference boundary. Positive ranks expand above it, while negative ranks contract below it.

# 2. Fractional-dimensional projections

The ordinary dimensional projections of a binary octave are:

$$
X_{1D}(n)=2^n
$$

$$
X_{2D}(n)=4^n=2^{2n}
$$

$$
X_{3D}(n)=8^n=2^{3n}
$$

We can extend this construction to fractional dimensions.

Define:

$$
\boxed{X_D(n)=2^{Dn}}
$$

where:

- $n$ is the octave position.
- $D$ is the dimensional exponent.
- $X_D$ is a dimensionless scale factor.

This gives us a continuous family of dimensional projections.

For example:

$$
D\in\{0.25,\ 0.5,\ 0.75,\ 1,\ 2,\ 3\}
$$

The fractional values are mathematical projections. They do not automatically describe ordinary Euclidean spaces with fractional numbers of spatial axes.

## 2.1 The four below-one reference projections

Take the negative third octave:

$$
n=-3
$$

The ordinary one-dimensional value is:

$$
X_1(-3)=2^{-3}=0.125
$$

Now apply fractional dimensional exponents.

| Dimension $D$ | Formula at $n=-3$ | Value | Name |
|---:|---:|---:|---|
| 0.25D | $2^{-0.75}$ | 0.5946035575 | Quarter-dimensional projection |
| 0.5D | $2^{-1.5}$ | 0.3535533906 | Half-dimensional projection |
| 0.75D | $2^{-2.25}$ | 0.2102241038 | Three-quarter-dimensional projection |
| 1D | $2^{-3}$ | 0.125 | Full linear projection |

The sequence is important:

$$
0.5946 > 0.3536 > 0.2102 > 0.125
$$

As the dimensional exponent increases, the same negative octave produces a smaller value.

This is a direct consequence of:

$$
X_D(-3)=2^{-3D}
$$

The fractional-dimensional projections therefore interpolate between unity and the full one-dimensional negative-rank value.

## 2.2 The same projections above unity

For comparison, take the positive third octave:

The positive and negative sides are reciprocal:

$$
\boxed{X_D(n)X_D(-n)=1}

This identity holds for every real $D$ and $n$.

It is one of the simplest ways to preserve symmetry across the unity boundary.

# 3. The logarithmic interpretation of 0.125

The logarithm converts multiplicative scale into an additive coordinate.

For the first-order scale:

$$
X_1(n)=2^n
$$

Taking the binary logarithm:

$$
\boxed{\log_2 X_1(n)=n}
$$

Therefore:

$$
\log_2(0.125)=-3
$$

This is the central synchronization:

**0.125 in ordinary scale space is exactly rank -3 in logarithmic space.**

The number is below unity in the original coordinate, but its logarithmic coordinate is an ordinary negative integer.

The same operation applies to fractional dimensions:

$$
\log_2 X_D(n)=Dn
$$

At $n=-3$:

| Dimension | Original scale | Logarithmic coordinate |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 0.25D | 0.5946035575 | -0.75 |
| 0.5D | 0.3535533906 | -1.5 |
| 0.75D | 0.2102241038 | -2.25 |
| 1D | 0.125 | -3 |

This is useful because fractional dimensions become fractional negative ranks.

The logarithmic representation makes their relationship much easier to compare.

# 4. Negative rank as reciprocal symmetry

The negative scale can be represented in two equivalent ways.

First, as a value below unity:

$$
x_-=2^{-r}
$$

Second, as the reciprocal of a positive-rank value:

$$
x_-=\frac{1}{2^r}
$$

where $r>0$.

For the third rank:

$$
0.125=2^{-3}=\frac{1}{8}
$$

This gives us a direct positive-negative correspondence:

| Positive rank | Positive value | Negative rank | Negative value |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| +1 | 2 | -1 | 0.5 |
| +2 | 4 | -2 | 0.25 |
| +3 | 8 | -3 | 0.125 |
| +4 | 16 | -4 | 0.0625 |
| +5 | 32 | -5 | 0.03125 |
| +6 | 64 | -6 | 0.015625 |
| +8 | 256 | -8 | 0.00390625 |

The reciprocal transformation is:

$$
\boxed{x(-n)=\frac{1}{x(n)}}
$$

The logarithmic transformation changes this reciprocal relationship into a sign reversal:

$$
\log_2\left(\frac1x\right)=-\log_2(x)
$$

This is why negative rank is naturally understood as the reciprocal side of positive rank.

It is not a separate arbitrary numerical system.

# 5. Higher-order logarithmic scale spaces

The first-order scale is:

$$
X_1(n)=2^n
$$

The higher-order transformations are defined recursively:

$$
X_2(n)=2^{X_1(n)}
$$

$$
X_3(n)=2^{X_2(n)}
$$

$$
X_4(n)=2^{X_3(n)}
$$

Or, explicitly:

$$
X_2(n)=2^{2^n}
$$

$$
X_3(n)=2^{2^{2^n}}
$$

$$
X_4(n)=2^{2^{2^{2^n}}}
$$

These are exponential coordinate spaces.

Their ordinary values are always greater than one. In particular:

$$
\lim_{n\to-\infty}X_k(n)=1
$$

for every positive order $k$.

This produces an important asymmetry:

- First-order negative ranks approach zero.
- Higher-order positive exponential lifts approach one.

Consequently, ordinary higher-order exponentiation does not preserve the original below-one scale.

We need a reciprocal branch if we want to retain negative-rank information.

---

# 6. Reciprocal exponential branches

Define the reciprocal higher-order scale:

$$
\boxed{X_k^-(r)=\frac{1}{X_k(r)}}
$$

where $r\geq0$ is the magnitude of the negative rank.

Equivalently:

$$
X_k^-(r)=2^{-X_{k-1}(r)}
$$

with:

$$
X_0(r)=r
$$

This gives:

$$
X_1^-(r)=2^{-r}
$$

$$
X_2^-(r)=2^{-2^r}
$$

$$
X_3^-(r)=2^{-2^{2^r}}
$$

$$
X_4^-(r)=2^{-2^{2^{2^r}}}
$$

The reciprocal branch preserves the below-one domain while allowing higher-order exponential transformations.

## 6.1 Example: negative rank three

Set:

$$
r=3
$$

The first-order reciprocal value is:

$$
X_1^-(3)=2^{-3}=0.125
$$

The second-order reciprocal value is:

$$
X_2^-(3)=2^{-8}=\frac1{256}
$$

The third-order reciprocal value is:

$$
X_3^-(3)=2^{-256}
$$

The fourth-order reciprocal value is:

$$
X_4^-(3)=2^{-65536}
$$

| Order | Positive scale | Reciprocal scale | Interpretation |
|---:|---:|---:|---|
| 1 | $2^3=8$ | $2^{-3}=0.125$ | Ordinary reciprocal octave |
| 2 | $2^8=256$ | $2^{-8}=1/256$ | First exponential reciprocal |
| 3 | $2^{256}$ | $2^{-256}$ | Second exponential reciprocal |
| 4 | $2^{65536}$ | $2^{-65536}$ | Third exponential reciprocal |

This creates a symmetric pair at every order:

$$
\boxed{X_k^+(r)X_k^-(r)=1}
$$

The positive and negative branches are reciprocal even when their numerical magnitudes become enormously different.

This is a mathematical construction for preserving the negative side of the scale hierarchy.

# 8. Logarithmic synchronization across orders

The logarithm reverses exponentiation.

For the first order:

$$
\log_2 X_1(n)=n
$$

For the second order:

$$
\log_2 X_2(n)=X_1(n)
$$

For the third order:

$$
\log_2 X_3(n)=X_2(n)
$$

For the fourth order:

$$
\log_2 X_4(n)=X_3(n)
$$

Thus, every logarithmic operation descends one level in the exponential hierarchy.

For reciprocal branches:

$$
\log_2 X_k^-(r)=-X_{k-1}(r)
$$

This is the synchronization rule.

The negative sign is retained by the reciprocal branch.

For example:

$$
\log_2(0.125)=-3
$$

But in the second-order reciprocal space:

$$
\log_2(1/256)=-8
$$

And in the third-order reciprocal space:

$$
\log_2(2^{-256})=-256
$$

The same negative direction is preserved, while the magnitude of the logarithmic coordinate is transformed.

## 8.1 Repeated logarithms

For a positive number $x$, define the repeated logarithm:

$$
\log_2^{[1]}(x)=\log_2(x)
$$

$$
\log_2^{[2]}(x)=\log_2(\log_2(x))
$$

$$
\log_2^{[3]}(x)=\log_2(\log_2(\log_2(x)))
$$

However, repeated logarithms have a domain restriction.

For $x=0.125$:

$$
\log_2(0.125)=-3
$$

The next real logarithm is undefined:

$$
\log_2(-3)\notin\mathbb R
$$

Therefore, ordinary repeated logarithms cannot directly continue through the negative coordinate.

The reciprocal exponential branch solves a different problem: it preserves negative-rank information by taking logarithms of positive reciprocal values.

For example:

$$
\log_2(2^{-256})=-256
$$

This gives a real-valued logarithmic coordinate at every order.

It is important not to confuse reciprocal logarithmic synchronization with unrestricted repeated logarithms.

# 10. Synchronization with base-4 rank

The binary octave uses:

$$

$$

The corresponding base-4 scale is:

$$
4^n=2^{2n}
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{4^n=(2^n)^2}
$$

The binary and base-4 systems are directly related through a factor of two in the logarithmic exponent.

For a fractional dimensional projection:

$$
X_D(n)=2^{Dn}
$$

Its base-4 logarithmic coordinate is:

$$
\log_4 X_D(n)=\frac{Dn}{2}

At $n=-3$:

| Dimension | Binary log rank | Base-4 log rank |
|---:|---:|---:|

| 0.5D | -1.5 | -0.75 |
| 0.75D | -2.25 | -1.125 |
| 1D | -3 | -1.5 |

The same scale therefore has different numerical coordinates depending on the logarithmic base.

This is not a contradiction. It is a change of coordinate system.

The conversion is exact:

$$
\log_4(x)=\frac12\log_2(x)

A useful Laegna representation is to retain both coordinates:

- Binary rank: octave subdivision.

# 15. Suggested computational implementation

A small Python implementation can calculate the first-order and fractional projections directly.

```python
import math

def scale(n, dimension=1.0):
    return 2.0 ** (dimension * n)


def log_rank(value, base=2.0):
    if value <= 0:
        raise ValueError("Logarithm requires a positive value")
    return math.log(value, base)


def reciprocal_order(rank, order=1, dimension=1.0):
    if rank < 0:
        raise ValueError("Use a non-negative rank magnitude")

    exponent = rank

    for _ in range(order - 1):
        exponent = 2.0 ** exponent

    return 2.0 ** (-dimension * exponent)


for dimension in (0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1.0):
    value = scale(-3, dimension)

    print({
        "dimension": dimension,
        "value": value,
        "binary_rank": log_rank(value)
    })
```

For very high orders, ordinary floating-point numbers will underflow or overflow. A robust implementation should preserve exponent towers symbolically, rather than attempting to evaluate them as ordinary floating-point values.

# 4. Inverse length as a relative coordinate

An important distinction is necessary between a physical inverse length and its normalized relative value.

| Representation | Formula | Unit | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Physical inverse length | $1/L_n$ | $\mathrm{m^{-1}}$ | Actual inverse physical length |
| Inverse centimetre | $100/L_n$ | $\mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ | Same quantity in centimetres |
| Inverse kilometre | $0.001/L_n$ | $\mathrm{km^{-1}}$ | Same quantity in kilometres |
| Normalized inverse | $\ell_P/L_n$ | Dimensionless | Relative inverse scale |
| Inverse Planck length | $1/\ell_P$ | $\mathrm{m^{-1}}$ | Fixed reference value |
| Inverse-square length | $1/L_n^2$ | $\mathrm{m^{-2}}$ | Inverse-square scale |

The normalized inverse is particularly simple:

$$
\boxed{
J_n^{(P)}=\frac{\ell_P}{L_n}=2^{-n}
}
$$

For example:

- Rank $-3$: inverse scale is $8$ times the Planck inverse reference.
- Rank $0$: inverse scale equals the Planck inverse reference.
- Rank $+3$: inverse scale is $1/8$ of the Planck inverse reference.

This makes the inverse coordinate directly compatible with the logarithmic rank system.

# 5. Dimensional projections and inverse powers

The direct scale can be projected into different dimensional exponents.

Define:

$$
F_D(n)=2^{Dn}
$$

where $D$ is the dimensional exponent.

For integer dimensions:

$$
F_1(n)=2^n
$$

$$
F_2(n)=4^n
$$

$$
F_3(n)=8^n
$$

The corresponding inverse projections are:

$$
F_D^{-1}(n)=2^{-Dn}
$$

These can be interpreted as normalized inverse length, inverse area, and inverse volume factors.

# 6. Inverse-square scale and curvature coordinates

The inverse-square scale is already part of the higher-order scale system.

For:

$$
L_n=\ell_P2^n
$$

we obtain:

$$
K_n=\frac{1}{L_n^2}
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
K_n=\ell_P^{-2}4^{-n}
}
$$

The opposite-rank relationship is:

$$
K_nK_{-n}=\ell_P^{-4}
$$

This is structurally parallel to the inverse-length relationship.

| Coordinate | Formula | Unit | Opposite-rank product |
|---|---|---|---|
| Direct length | $L_n=\ell_P2^n$ | $\mathrm m$ | $\ell_P^2$ |
| Inverse length | $J_n=\ell_P^{-1}2^{-n}$ | $\mathrm{m^{-1}}$ | $\ell_P^{-2}$ |
| Direct area factor | $A_n=\ell_P^2 4^n$ | $\mathrm{m^2}$ | $\ell_P^4$ |
| Inverse area factor | $K_n=\ell_P^{-2}4^{-n}$ | $\mathrm{m^{-2}}$ | $\ell_P^{-4}$ |
| Direct volume factor | $V_n=\ell_P^3 8^n$ | $\mathrm{m^3}$ | $\ell_P^6$ |
| Inverse volume factor | $W_n=\ell_P^{-3}8^{-n}$ | $\mathrm{m^{-3}}$ | $\ell_P^{-6}$ |

The products in the final column are constant for every opposite-rank pair.

This gives a family of reciprocal invariants:

$$
L_nL_{-n}=\ell_P^2
$$

$$
J_nJ_{-n}=\ell_P^{-2}
$$

$$
A_nA_{-n}=\ell_P^4
$$

$$
K_nK_{-n}=\ell_P^{-4}
$$

$$
V_nV_{-n}=\ell_P^6
$$

$$
W_nW_{-n}=\ell_P^{-6}
$$

These are consequences of the power-law definitions, not independent physical laws.

# 7. Inverse time and frequency

The same construction applies to time.

The direct temporal scale is:

$$
T_n=t_P2^n
$$

Its inverse is:

$$
f_n=\frac{1}{T_n}
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
f_n=t_P^{-1}2^{-n}
}
$$

The Planck time is approximately:

$$
t_P=5.391247\times10^{-44}\ \mathrm s
$$

The inverse Planck time is:

$$
t_P^{-1}\approx1.855\times10^{43}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}
$$

| Rank | Time factor $2^n$ | Inverse-time factor $2^{-n}$ | Inverse time ($\mathrm{s^{-1}}$) |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|

| -2 | 1/4 | 4 | $7.420\times10^{43}$ |

| +3 | 8 | 1/8 | $2.319\times10^{42}$ |

Inverse time has units of frequency, but it should not automatically be identified with an observed oscillation frequency. It is the reciprocal of the characteristic time scale.

## 7.1. Space-time inverse relationship

Because:

$$
\ell_P=ct_P
$$

we have:

$$
\frac{1}{\ell_P}=\frac{1}{ct_P}
$$

Consequently:

$$
\frac{J_n}{f_n} = \frac{t_P}{\ell_P} = \frac{1}{c}
$$

Or equivalently:

$$
\boxed{
\frac{f_n}{J_n}=c
}
$$

The direct scales also satisfy:

$$
\frac{L_n}{T_n}=c
$$

Thus the direct and inverse representations preserve the same light-speed relationship.

This produces dimensionless coordinates.

Examples:

$$

# 10. Reciprocal coordinates as a dual scale axis

The direct and inverse representations can be viewed as two opposing coordinate axes.

Direct scale:

$$
L_n=\ell_P2^n
$$

Inverse scale:

$$
J_n=\ell_P^{-1}2^{-n}
$$

Their product is:

$$
\boxed{L_nJ_n=1}
$$

This is the defining reciprocal identity.

The logarithmic coordinates make the opposition especially clear:

$$
\log_2\left(\frac{L_n}{\ell_P}\right)=n
$$

$$
\log_2\left(\frac{J_n}{\ell_P^{-1}}\right)=-n
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
\rho_J=-\rho_L
}
$$

The inverse coordinate is the reflection of the direct coordinate around rank zero.

This does not mean that inverse length is a second physical space. It means that the same scale can be represented by two complementary logarithmic coordinates.

# 11. Higher-dimensional inverse projections

The inverse-dimensional construction can be generalized.

For dimensional exponent $D$:

$$
F_D(n)=2^{Dn}
$$

The inverse projection is:

$$
F_D^{-1}(n)=2^{-Dn}
$$

The physical version is:

$$
J_D(n)=\ell_P^{-D}2^{-Dn}
$$

For integer $D$:

| Dimension | Direct scale | Inverse scale | Unit |
|---:|---|---|---|
| 1 | $\ell_P2^n$ | $\ell_P^{-1}2^{-n}$ | $\mathrm{m^{-1}}$ |
| 2 | $\ell_P^2 4^n$ | $\ell_P^{-2}4^{-n}$ | $\mathrm{m^{-2}}$ |
| 3 | $\ell_P^3 8^n$ | $\ell_P^{-3}8^{-n}$ | $\mathrm{m^{-3}}$ |
| 4 | $\ell_P^4 16^n$ | $\ell_P^{-4}16^{-n}$ | $\mathrm{m^{-4}}$ |

This provides a consistent family of dimensional scale projections.

For fractional $D$, the same equations remain mathematically valid as exponent-based interpolation:

$$
J_D(n)=\ell_P^{-D}2^{-Dn}
$$

However, a fractional exponent does not by itself establish a physical fractional spatial dimension. A physical interpretation would require a model defining the relevant measure, geometry, or scaling law.

# 12. Inverse scale and logarithmic linearization

The direct scale can be linearized using a logarithm:

$$
n=\log_2\left(\frac{L_n}{\ell_P}\right)
$$

The inverse scale can be linearized as:

$$
-n=\log_2\left(\frac{J_n}{\ell_P^{-1}}\right)
$$

This gives two symmetric logarithmic coordinates.

A direct scale grows exponentially with rank, while its inverse decreases exponentially.

In logarithmic coordinates, both become linear.

This is useful for:

- comparing widely separated physical scales;
- constructing symmetric tables around a reference;
- converting multiplicative relationships into additive rank differences;
- distinguishing direct and reciprocal scaling;
- extending the same coordinate structure to area, volume, time, and other quantities.

The important feature is that reciprocal symmetry survives logarithmic linearization.

# 14. Important physical distinctions

The inverse-scale system is mathematically consistent, but several distinctions must remain explicit.

### 14.1. Inverse length is not automatically frequency

An inverse length has units of $\mathrm{m^{-1}}$.

A frequency has units of $\mathrm{s^{-1}}$.

They can be related through the speed of light:

$$
f=cJ
$$

for a light wave under the appropriate wavelength-frequency convention.

But they are not the same physical quantity.

### 14.2. Inverse-square length is not automatically curvature

The quantity:

$$
K=\frac{1}{L^2}
$$

has the correct dimensions for a curvature scale.

However, actual geometric curvature depends on the metric and geometry of the system. An inverse-square characteristic length is not automatically a measured curvature invariant.

### 14.3. Inverse volume is not automatically density

The quantity:

$$
W=\frac{1}{L^3}
$$

has inverse-volume units.

It can be used as a characteristic volume density factor, but a physical mass density or energy density also requires a mass or energy quantity.

### 14.4. Inverse Planck length and inverse Planck mass are different

The inverse Planck length has units:

$$
[\ell_P^{-1}]=\mathrm{m^{-1}}
$$

The inverse Planck mass has units:

$$
[m_P^{-1}]=\mathrm{kg^{-1}}
$$

They must not be mixed.

In natural units, physical dimensions can be related through constants such as $c$ and $\hbar$, but their SI representations remain different.

# 15. Final synthesis

The direct scale system and the inverse scale system are two complementary descriptions of the same hierarchy.

The direct coordinate is:

$$
L_n=\ell_P2^n
$$

The inverse coordinate is:

$$
J_n=\ell_P^{-1}2^{-n}
$$

The normalized inverse coordinate is:

$$
J_n^{(P)}=2^{-n}
$$

The inverse-square coordinate is:

$$
K_n=\ell_P^{-2}4^{-n}
$$

The inverse-volume coordinate is:

$$
W_n=\ell_P^{-3}8^{-n}
$$

The inverse-time coordinate is:

$$
f_n=t_P^{-1}2^{-n}
$$

All of these share the same underlying rank structure.

The key reciprocal identities are:

$$
L_nJ_n=1
$$

$$
L_nL_{-n}=\ell_P^2
$$

$$
J_nJ_{-n}=\ell_P^{-2}
$$

$$
K_nK_{-n}=\ell_P^{-4}
$$

The resulting interpretation is:

- Direct ranks describe extension.
- Negative direct ranks describe scales below the reference.
- Inverse ranks describe reciprocal extension.
- Dimensional projections extend the scale into area, volume, and higher powers.
- Dimensionless ratios make different physical units comparable.
- Reciprocal rank pairs provide constant-product symmetry.
- Logarithmic coordinates convert these multiplicative relationships into linear rank relationships.

**Laegna inverse scale physics therefore adds a second, reciprocal coordinate system to the existing scale hierarchy.**

It does not replace the direct scale tables. It makes their inverse structure explicit, measurable, and reusable across physical units and dimensional projections.
